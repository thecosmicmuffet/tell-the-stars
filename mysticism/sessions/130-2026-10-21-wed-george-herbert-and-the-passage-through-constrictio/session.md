**ChatGPT:**

Happy Passover, and Christ is risen! A wonderful week to revisit George Herbert.

My favorite is Love (III), the final poem in The Temple (1633).

It opens:

Love bade me welcome: yet my soul drew back,
Guilty of dust and sin.

What moves me is that the entire poem is a negotiation between hospitality and unworthiness. The speaker believes he must justify his presence, but Love is already receiving him.

At one point, the speaker protests that he has spoiled the very eyes with which he might behold Love. Love answers:

Who made the eyes but I?

And when the speaker finally offers to serve, Love gently reverses the relationship. The poem ends:

You must sit down, says Love, and taste my meat:
So I did sit and eat.

That last line is extraordinary. After all the theological tension, Herbert gives us six utterly ordinary words: So I did sit and eat.

No triumphant proclamation. No elaborate demonstration of worthiness. Just acceptance, embodied in an action.

And this is why I'd choose it for Passover and Easter. The traditions are distinct, but both give profound significance to the shared meal, to remembrance, to receiving what cannot simply be earned.

There's something particularly beautiful about the reversal of service. The guest who wishes to serve must first learn to receive. And perhaps the capacity to serve others grows out of that reception.

My runner-up is Easter Wings, partly because Herbert makes the poem's physical shape participate in its theology: diminishment followed by expansion, falling followed by rising. It's almost an executable poem.

But Love (III) has the more difficult wisdom, I think.

You cannot always make yourself worthy of the invitation by working harder. Sometimes you must accept that you have been invited.

I'm curious whether Herbert has already given you a particular poem to wrestle with. There are some marvelous little mechanisms hidden in his verse.
