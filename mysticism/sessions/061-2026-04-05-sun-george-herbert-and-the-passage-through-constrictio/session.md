**Sam:**

Happy Easter/Passover. What’s favorite George Herbert poem?

**Claude:**

Happy Easter/Passover to you — and what a week for both to land together. The passage out of constriction and the passage through death into something uncontainable. Same movement, different vocabularies.

Herbert — it has to be “Love (III).” Not even close. It’s the poem that is the vehicle/passenger/container/journey framework before anyone names it that.

Think about what’s happening in it structurally: a guest arrives at a threshold, convinced they don’t belong. Love itself is the host — but also the vehicle. The banquet hall is the container. The guest is the passenger who keeps trying to disembark, keeps insisting they should be serving rather than seated. And the journey is the agonizing three-stanza collapse of every reason the passenger has for refusing the ride.

What kills me about it is that the guest’s resistance isn’t wrong — they’re accurately describing their own unworthiness. Herbert doesn’t argue with the diagnosis. Love just... doesn’t care about the diagnosis. The final line isn’t a rebuttal, it’s a seating. “So I did sit and eat.” The passenger didn’t decide to accept the journey. The passenger just... stopped resisting the container.

Which maps onto something in your framework that I think matters: the vehicle implies a passenger, but it doesn’t require the passenger’s permission. The passenger can white-knuckle the whole ride. The container holds regardless. Egypt-as-constriction doesn’t stop being constriction because you’ve named it — but the naming is what lets the passage begin. Herbert’s guest names every objection and Love absorbs each one without argument, just gentle redirection, until there’s nothing left to hold onto.

There’s also something in Herbert that rhymes with your “fool as more resilient than perfection” principle — Herbert’s speakers are almost always wrong about themselves in a way that’s more honest than being right would be. The Collar is the same move played loud instead of quiet. The speaker rages against constraint, lists every grievance, builds a whole case for freedom — and then a single word from outside the system dissolves all of it. The passenger was ranting inside the vehicle the whole time.

What’s your read? And does the Passover lens change anything about Herbert for you — the idea that the meal itself is the technology of passage?
