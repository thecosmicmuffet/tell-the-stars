The reversal you’ve found

חֶרֶב (cherev) - sword - is grammatically feminine. Despite lacking the feminine ending, it takes feminine verbs and adjectives.

אֵת (et) - ploughshare - is grammatically masculine.

So the swords-to-ploughshares is also feminine-to-masculine. The desolating, drying instrument (grammatically she) becomes the cutting implement that opens for seed (grammatically he).

This inverts the expected polarity. War is feminine; cultivation is masculine.

The Akkadian connection

Here’s something remarkable from the search: cherev means “sword” and Akkadian harbu is a kind of plow. 
Balashon

The same root-sound in an older Semitic language means plow. So the Hebrew “sword” may carry within it the memory of being a ploughshare all along. The drying-desolating function differentiated later from the cutting-opening function, but they were once the same word for the same action.

The charash field - silence and plowing

חָרַשׁ (charash) is extraordinary. This single root means:

To plow (scratch the earth)
To engrave (scratch stone/metal)
To be silent
To be deaf
To devise secretly
To fabricate/craft
The craftsman that scratches, or engraves, in wood, stone or metal. 
Bible Hub

The plowman and the silent one are the same word. To plow is to hold your tongue. The craft emerges from what is not said.

The connection scholars note as “unknown” between silence and plowing may be this: the craftsman works without speaking. The furrow is made in concentration, in held breath. The word emerges from the work, not the mouth.

Charmer?

Not directly in charash, but the silence/secrecy cluster points toward it. The one who devises secretly while appearing silent. The one whose craft is not announced.
