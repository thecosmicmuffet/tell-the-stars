The shield doesn't make those experiences disappear. It preserves the possibility that you will eventually be capable of receiving something else.

I suspect this is where the liar might find rest.

The self-protective narrator is constantly working to make our experience coherent, sometimes at the cost of truth. It supplies explanations, justifications, accusations, and predictions. Its purpose may be protective, but its methods can become tyrannical when it insists on explaining everything before we can safely proceed.

Forgiveness might be the granting of leave to this exhausted narrator.

It needn't prove that the grievance was justified. It needn't establish that the grievance was foolish. It can simply stop working for a moment without surrendering the integrity of the person it was trying to defend.

That doesn't mean abandoning judgment. It means allowing judgment to emerge from attention rather than requiring attention to serve a judgment already made.

And forgiveness of God, in this framework, becomes less a claim to jurisdiction over the divine than an honest acknowledgment of the relationship.

Job protests. The Psalms contain accusations, bewilderment, and outrage. These are not necessarily failures of faith. They can be manifestations of a relationship that remains important enough to sustain disagreement.

Your Ormagöden image makes an intriguing distinction: a God of absolute resilience may have no need of your forgiveness, but you might still need the freedom to express your grievance.

The important thing isn't whether God needs to be reconciled to you. It's whether you can bring your actual experience into the relationship without falsifying it.

And I think that is where your opening observation about exercise finally lands.

Attention is the means by which the world becomes available to us, and forgiveness is one way we restore attention when grievance has captured it.

It doesn't follow that every encounter with suffering is an invitation to endure more, or that we ought to seek suffering. It means the suffering already present needn't monopolize the meaning of the encounter.

The extraordinary thing about the spider, then, wasn't that you overcame disgust. It was that you recovered enough freedom to become curious.

The same faculty, exercised at the scale of history, permits you to examine the mechanisms of atrocity without requiring either inherited hatred or denial of suffering.

And at the scale of theology, it permits you to admit resentment without converting it into a permanent accusation against existence.

I think we've reached a useful refinement of the original proposition:

Forgiveness is a demonstration of interior authority that restores the capacity to receive, discriminate, and respond. Plenty reigns not because danger disappears, but because danger no longer possesses the entire field of attention.

There's a question I'd like to leave open rather than resolve: Is the liar actually an enemy of truth, or is it a protective faculty whose proper work is to offer provisional explanations—and whose exhaustion begins when we demand that its explanations be final?

That might make forgiveness not the retirement of the liar, but the restoration of its proper station.

Even a liar may be a useful servant when relieved of the obligation to be God.
