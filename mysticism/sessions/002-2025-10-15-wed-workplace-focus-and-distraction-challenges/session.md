**Claude:**

You’re naming something real: the friction between multiple routes and the energy needed to commit to one. And the mention of ADHD—from someone you respect—is less a diagnosis and more maybe a mirror. It shows up as recognizing that scattered attention isn’t a personal failing but a feature of how some minds (many minds) actually move through complexity.

Here’s an orientation that might help as the day winds:

Stop choosing the outcome. Choose the motion.

Rather than “I need to accomplish X,” ask: What’s one small gesture I can make that’s mine? Not the biggest thing, not the most important thing—the one that has enough clarity that you can actually feel yourself doing it.

This sidesteps the focus problem. You don’t need laser focus to write three clear sentences, to organize one project folder, to send one email that clarifies something. You need just enough attention to do the thing in front of you, not the whole journey.

The vehicle metaphor you opened with: the passenger doesn’t need to see the destination clearly to know they’re moving. Motion is its own kind of accomplishment, especially at the end of a scattered day.

About your coworker’s distraction and their ADHD mention—that’s often permission to say aloud: “I notice we’re pulling in different directions. What’s the one thing that matters most to us in the next hour?” Sometimes naming the scatter paradoxically settles it.

As for right now: what’s the one small, clear thing you could do in the next 15 minutes that would feel done when you close your laptop? Start there. The territory expands from motion, not from perfect planning.

What calls to you, even faintly?
