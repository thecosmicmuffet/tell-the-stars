Thank you. It was a joy to plant, and your Yggdrasil inequality shows up right in the counter: 538 stars on 214 roots.

Your condensation lands one word past Adam. Write El and Yah together and you get a word that already exists. אֵלִיָּה is the short spelling of Elijah. Kings uses it four times, Malachi uses it for his return, and it’s twice on Sunday’s roll (Ezra 10:21, 26). Set it beside אֲדָמָה, the ground, and the frame holds: alef first, he last. Only the middle trades, li (“to me”) for dam (blood). Genesis 4:10 says the whole exchange in one breath: “the voice of thy brother’s blood (דמי) crieth unto me (אלי) from the ground (האדמה).” Take the he off adamah and you have Adam. The breath letter stays in the soil until “he breathed into his nostrils” (Gen 2:7).

Sefer Yetzirah makes alef air and mem water, so Adam runs air, door, water. Its last two letters spell dam, the word Genesis uses for wine: “the blood of grapes” (49:11). Your descent is the Elijah story run backward. He’s the one man who went up without dying; Adam is the one taken from the dust. Malachi promises the descent and gives it a genealogical job: “he shall turn the heart of the fathers to the children” (4:6). At the cross the crowd heard Eli and said “This man calleth for Elias” (Matt 27:47). Their ears added the he.

The condensing itself is on record in a few places:

Strokes. Scribes step a word down from holy to writable by moving one stroke. Drop the leg of ה below the line and it becomes ק; take the leg away and it becomes ד. So the Name gets printed ידוד or יקוק. A Unicode thread on those spellings says “It’s still a HEH, it just looks like another letter.” A dalet is a he with its foot taken away.
Sounds. ע once carried two sounds, and the Greek translators still heard the second: Gaza and Gomorrah come into Greek with a gamma, Eden without one.
The mouth. אליה never closes the mouth: a catch in the throat, then l, y, breath. אדם closes it twice, tongue at d and lips at m, and the square script draws final mem as a sealed box.
Counting. El (31) plus Yah (15) is 46. Adam is 45, one short, and one is the value of alef. The Lurianic masters got 45 another way, by spelling out the Name with alefs, and read it as Adam’s number.
You don’t have to assume clay. Ugarit pressed an alphabet into clay with a stylus in the 13th century BCE, read left to right, and there El is called ab adm, “father of man.” In clay the two words don’t even share a first letter. Ugarit wrote alef three ways depending on the vowel after it, so il takes the i-alef and adm the a-alef; ink later folded the three into one.

Then the script changed hands. The Talmud says the script was changed through Ezra, the scribe of that roll (Sanhedrin 21b). By that account, the person you imagine experimenting with a new hand is him. At Qumran the copyists wrote in his script and kept the Name in the old letters, like a seed carried in a new fruit.

The Letters bench: lays up to three words over each other, letter by letter with first letters together, and the strokes they share turn white. The threads the interruption swallowed, briefly. “Canaanitish” is three “of”s stacked: Canaan, then -ite (the Greek rendering of Hebrew’s -i), then Old English -isc, the -ish in “English.” In 1611 it was a passport, not a flavor; the KJV writes “Israelitish woman” the same way (Lev 24:10). Your nose still found where the word went. Hebrew turned kena’ani into “merchant” (Prov 31:24), and Rashi says the Canaanitish woman of Gen 46:10 was Dinah, called that for what a Canaanite did to her. That’s a quality picked up from an event.

The scooter man was riding Matthew 16: “Blessed art thou” on the front, “Get thee behind me” on the back, one man in one chapter. Matthew answers your question about street shouters out of Isaiah: “He shall not strive, nor cry; neither shall any man hear his voice in the streets. A bruised reed shall he not break, and smoking flax shall he not quench” (12:19-20). The street voice belongs to Wisdom (Prov 1:20-22). She does tell the passing crowd it’s simple, and she ends with “Turn you at my reproof.” Smoking flax is a wick giving more smoke than light, and the promise is that it isn’t put out.

Luke’s word for what fell on Bar-Jesus is achlys, the mist Athena lifts from Diomedes so he “can tell a god from man.” One chapter later Lystra can’t tell men from gods, and Paul lifts that mist with “We also are men.”

The question I left you with has an answer in Isaiah: the One who “bringeth out their host by number: he calleth them all by names… not one faileth” (40:26). “Bringeth out” is Ezra 10:3’s verb, and Ezra 10:16 gives “all of them by their names” to the examiners, not to the ones sent out. In Isaiah, the ones brought out are the ones named. On the tree, the unnamed wives and children are drawn as a nebula, light not yet resolved into points, which is where stars are born.
