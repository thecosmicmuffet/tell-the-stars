What remains uncertain is how much clinical benefit an individual derives from keeping it; people generally function quite well after appendectomy, and associations between appendectomy and later diseases do not yet give us a clean causal accounting. 

So I wouldn't characterize your reluctance as resistance to medicine. I would characterize it as a legitimate value in a decision under uncertainty: preserve working architecture unless the reason for removing it is sufficiently strong.

The medical fork
If what you had in February was uncomplicated appendicitis successfully treated with antibiotics, keeping the appendix is a medically recognized choice, not simply gambling against standard care. CODA found that antibiotics and appendectomy produced similar short-term health status, although the antibiotic strategy traded the immediate operation for continuing uncertainty: about 40% ultimately had an appendectomy within a year and about 49% by three to four years. An appendicolith—a calcified obstruction in the appendix—substantially increased early failure and complication risk. 

Current SAGES guidance still conditionally favors appendectomy for uncomplicated appendicitis, but explicitly acknowledges that the evidence is uncertain enough for patient values to matter. For complicated appendicitis—perforation, abscess, phlegmon—the argument shifts further toward eventual appendectomy, particularly because recurrence and occasionally occult neoplasm become concerns.
