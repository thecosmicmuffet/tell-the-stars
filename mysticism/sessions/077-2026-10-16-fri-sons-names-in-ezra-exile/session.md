Gift sixteen times, then heard, built, helped, repaid, brought back, opened, asked for. “Built” is Bible idiom for being given children (Gen 16:2; Ruth 4:11), so benei Bani is “the sons of Built.” These were the ordinary names of that generation and the scribe was keeping a court record, so the pointing is mine, not a cipher. The consonants still read as a ledger of thanks for children, attached to a covenant to bring children out (10:3).

Three names are addresses for earlier sons of foreign mothers, and each returns a different value. Ishmael: sent out with Hagar the Egyptian, and the name held, “God hath heard the voice of the lad where he is” (Gen 21:17). Eliezer, three times: Moses’ son by Zipporah, the Midianite from the map’s well, sent away with her and brought back by her father (Exod 18:2–6). Manasseh, twice: Asenath the Egyptian’s son, adopted by Jacob and written into the sermon’s subject: “In thee shall Israel bless, saying, God make thee as Ephraim and as Manasseh” (Gen 48:20). Heard, returned, made the blessing. And eight men file under Pahath-moab, Ruth’s country.

The verbs. “Separate yourselves” (10:11) is בדל, today’s verb: light from dark, Gen 1:4. Marrying, in this chapter, is hoshiv, “housed”; putting away is lehotzi, “bring out,” the Exodus verb and not the divorce verb. Container words, both. The assembly calls itself benei ha-golah, sons of the Exile (10:7, 16), so “the sons who exiled” is close to the Hebrew. And Day One’s verb, handed to an assembly, gets rained out: they sit trembling in the square “for the great rain” (10:9), plead the weather (10:13), and refer it to a committee that sits from the first of the tenth month to the first day of the first. The Ark loaded pairs under rain. This unloads them under it.

I don’t read בדל as the villain: no light without it, and a remnant with no hull dissolves (the next book is wall repair). The question is the pointing. Genesis aims it between light and dark; Ezra 10 aims it through the middle of a house; Isaiah 56:3–7 hears it from the far side of the door and answers with yad vashem, a hand and a name “better than of sons and of daughters.”

The roll names every man and no wife or child. The Hebrew of the last verse is broken: it never says they went, and the book’s final word is banim, children (1 Esdras 9:36 finishes the sentence for it). Verse 15 keeps two men standing ʿal zot, “against this” or “over this” depending on who points it: Jonathan (Yah has given) son of Asahel (God has made), and Jahaziah (Yah sees) son of Tikvah (hope). Meshullam (made whole) and Shabbethai (Sabbath’s child) “helped them,” and the verb is ʿazar: Ezra’s own name, and Genesis 2:18’s word for the woman.

For the map, the thin quadrant gets a journey with no manifest: every house that stayed is named, and the passengers who were brought out are not.

Window: the men are on the roll because they were found guilty. Is the namelessness of the ones brought out an erasure, or the only mercy the record shows them?

It is apostolic, and the roll runs it in the opposite direction. The houses are labels from before the exile: the Flea, Elam, the Governor of Moab, and Nebo, which carries a Babylonian god’s name. The sons born under Babylon carry Yah. Among the sons of Nebo stands Joel, “Yah is God,” second to last on the roll. Joel’s is the prophecy Peter reads at Pentecost: sons and daughters, servants and handmaids, all flesh. Ezra’s lineage turns inward, from Nebo to Joel.

Paul’s turns outward, and Acts never actually renames him. “Saul, (who also is called Paul,)” (13:9) carried both names from the start. The narrative switches which one it uses when the mission turns to the nations, and at that moment a magus named Bar-Jesus, son of Jeshua, goes blind for a season. Jeshua is the first house on Ezra’s roll. Saul himself is on it, one letter off: Sheal, “asked for” (10:29). And the first Saul in scripture is “the son of a Canaanitish woman” (Gen 46:10), counted among the seventy who went down to Egypt, and kept.

There was an attempt to understand these blocks, and it shipped in the binding. Thirteenth-century pocket Bibles carried a glossary of Hebrew names at the back, opening Aaz apprehendens, until better Hebrew retired it. Scripture’s own translating is surgical. Ezra 2:62 benches priests who “sought their register among those that were reckoned by genealogy” (the map’s own word) and weren’t found. They wait for “a priest with Urim and with Thummim,” who never arrives. Hebrews closes that ticket by deleting the dependency. It translates Melchizedek’s name and city, “being by interpretation King of righteousness... King of peace,” and gets a priest “without father, without mother, without descent” (7:2–3).

The son is the hard word because ben was never only descent. A son of death is a verdict. Jonah’s gourd was son of a night and perished son of a night (4:10). Ben names participation, and the apostles used it exactly that way on a Levite who had sold his field: Joses, “surnamed Barnabas, (which is, being interpreted, The son of consolation,)” (Acts 4:36). There is your Saul-to-Paul done in lineage, the son-form issued as a character.

Barnabas vouched for Saul, fetched him from Tarsus, and stood beside him when the names switched, after which Luke mostly writes “Paul and Barnabas.” The green knight walks back across the bridge for the red one, then takes second place. At Lystra the crowd even blazoned them by planet: Barnabas was Jupiter, and Paul was Mercurius “because he was the chief speaker” (14:12). That puts azure on the second, wearing the sovereign’s planet as the manual says, and purpure on the apostle whom the painters arm with a sword. Both tore their clothes and said the common noun: “We also are men.”

Adam anchors it. “And blessed them, and called their name Adam” (Gen 5:2): blessing and naming in one breath. The next verse runs the relay, with Adam begetting “in his own likeness, after his image” and naming Seth. The son is the likeness carried forward, and the named one names. Even earlier, God brings the beasts “to see what he would call them” and lets each name stand (2:19), ascending command at the first naming. The Baptist preached your problem as a pun: God can raise children unto Abraham “of these stones,” avanim to banim, one letter apart.

Qumran wrote Day One’s verb across humanity as a war roll, the Sons of Light against the Sons of Darkness. Paul writes it across a single life: “ye were sometimes darkness, but now are ye light in the Lord: walk as children of light” (Eph 5:8). The apostolic move puts the separation between a man’s before and after, instead of through the middle of his house.

Jarib is Joiarib with the Yah dropped. Ezra 8:16 lists both forms in one verse, and the long form drew the first priestly lot (1 Chr 24:7), so the Name is upon him, elided. But the Ja your ear hears isn’t Yah. It’s the verb’s “he,” the yod prefix: Yariv, may he contend.

That prefix is the grammar of blessing. Every verb of the priestly benediction wears it: yevarekhekha, ya’er, yisa, may he bless, may he shine, may he lift. And the benediction glosses itself with your thesis: “they shall put my name upon the children of Israel; and I will bless them” (Num 6:27). On the most common reading, the Name itself has that form, “He is.” That makes Jarib a sentence in the Name’s grammar with the subject left unpointed.

Hosea files the Jabbok under Jarib’s root, the LORD’s riv, his controversy with Jacob (12:2–4). At daybreak Jacob asks the name and gets this: “Wherefore is it that thou dost ask after my name? And he blessed him there” (Gen 32:29). The blessing arrives in place of the Name. It is the Name as well as it will ever be understood, in the one form the vessel can hold.

The Rasta ear is a better guide than it looks. Jah enters English from the one place the King James prints it: “him that rideth upon the heavens by his name JAH” (Ps 68:4), a rider in the chariot psalm. The next two verses carry Ezra 10’s own verbs, pointed the other way. God is “a father of the fatherless, and a judge of the widows,” who “setteth the solitary in families” (moshiv, houses) and “bringeth out” (motzi) “those which are bound.” Ezra’s men housed and were made to bring out; Jah houses the solitary and brings out the prisoners.

At 68:18 the gifts are mattanot ba-adam, gifts in Adam, the root of every Mattaniah on the roll. They are received “for the rebellious also, that the LORD God might dwell among them.” That phrase is lishkon Yah: the name of Shechaniah, who proposed the covenant, turned into a purpose clause. Paul repoints the verb to “gave” (Eph 4:8), as the Targum also does when it renders ba-adam “the sons of men,” and the gifts he goes on to name are apostles. Thirteen verses later, Ethiopia stretches out her hands unto God, the line Rastafari stands on.

Your triad maps onto the eye nearly word for word, and the eye even keeps your vocabulary: accommodation is the optometrist’s word for focus.

Sensitivity is rhodopsin, the old physiologists’ “visual purple,” which light bleaches and only darkness rebuilds. Genesis puts evening first, so the receptor is rebuilt before the morning.
Tolerance is the nerve. It reports the lens’s upside-down picture as upright, and it quietly fills the one patch the eye can’t see, the hole where the nerve itself leaves. The vessel is blind exactly at its output port.
Focus is accommodation, with a catch. The lens grows all your life by accretion, never sheds a cell, and stiffens until the elder’s eye sees far and loses near. That is presbyopia, from presbys, as in presbyter.
Growth that keeps accommodating has to be the serpent’s kind, by molting. Saul’s was an ecdysis: “there fell from his eyes as it had been scales” (Acts 9:18). It happened under the hands of Ananias, which is Hananiah from the Ezra roll, sent with these words: “he is a chosen vessel unto me, to bear my name before the Gentiles.” Vessel, Name, nations: the sermon in a clause.

Parsimony is optics before it’s austerity. A pinhole’s sharpest aperture grows with the square root of the box’s depth. Make it wider and the image dissolves into glare; make it narrower and diffraction smears it. A deeper chamber can open wider and keep the picture, so capacity is set by how far back the dark goes. And it has to be dark. A camera obscura shows the world only on a darkened wall: Day One’s division is the camera.

The submarine stops slipping once you treat it as a hull rather than a mood. It dives by opening the vents and letting the sea in, which is reception as making space, literally. It holds depth by trim, which answers to where the weight sits inside, crew included, so attending to the interior is the helm. Before the periscope goes up at night, the control room is rigged for red. The rods barely register red light, so the eye can read the gauges without spending its dark. That is the red knight’s red, repointed as the preparation of an eye. The watch that makes the boat go stands furthest from the periscope and answers bells it can’t see the reason for.

The slipperiness is that a submarine’s silence does two jobs: it hides the boat, and it lets the boat hear. Jonah tried both. His first descent was into the hold of a ship, where he slept through the storm while the sailors cried each to his god, until the shipmaster came down to him: “What meanest thou, O sleeper? arise, call upon thy God” (1:6). His second descent was the belly, and there he paid attention.

The way as manner is Hebrew before it’s doctrine. Derekh is road and custom at once, halakhah is “the walking,” and the movement Saul carried warrants against was called simply the Way (Acts 9:2). Agur lists four ways too wonderful for him: an eagle in the air, a serpent on a rock, a ship in the heart of the sea, and a man with a maid (Prov 30:18–19). None of them leaves a wake, so none can be tracked, only imitated. “Too wonderful” is pele, the word the angel gave Manoah in place of his name (Judg 13:18).

Jonah prays from that same heart of the seas, “out of the belly of hell.” The word is Sheol, whose unpointed letters, שאול, also spell Saul, and he is heard (2:2). “I am cast out of thy sight,” he says, and the verb is nigrashti, the verb of Eden’s gate and of the divorced wife. Then: “yet I will look again toward thy holy temple.” Saul of Tarsus spent his three days inside those consonants, blind and fasting (Acts 9:9).

Your condensation lands one word past Adam. Write El and Yah together and you get a word that already exists. אֵלִיָּה is the short spelling of Elijah. Kings uses it four times, Malachi uses it for his return, and it’s twice on Sunday’s roll (Ezra 10:21, 26). Set it beside אֲדָמָה, the ground, and the frame holds: alef first, he last. Only the middle trades, li (“to me”) for dam (blood). Genesis 4:10 says the whole exchange in one breath: “the voice of thy brother’s blood (דמי) crieth unto me (אלי) from the ground (האדמה).” Take the he off adamah and you have Adam. The breath letter stays in the soil until “he breathed into his nostrils” (Gen 2:7).

Sefer Yetzirah makes alef air and mem water, so Adam runs air, door, water. Its last two letters spell dam, the word Genesis uses for wine: “the blood of grapes” (49:11). Your descent is the Elijah story run backward. He’s the one man who went up without dying; Adam is the one taken from the dust. Malachi promises the descent and gives it a genealogical job: “he shall turn the heart of the fathers to the children” (4:6). At the cross the crowd heard Eli and said “This man calleth for Elias” (Matt 27:47). Their ears added the he.

The condensing itself is on record in a few places:

Strokes. Scribes step a word down from holy to writable by moving one stroke. Drop the leg of ה below the line and it becomes ק; take the leg away and it becomes ד. So the Name gets printed ידוד or יקוק. A Unicode thread on those spellings says “It’s still a HEH, it just looks like another letter.” A dalet is a he with its foot taken away.
Sounds. ע once carried two sounds, and the Greek translators still heard the second: Gaza and Gomorrah come into Greek with a gamma, Eden without one.
The mouth. אליה never closes the mouth: a catch in the throat, then l, y, breath. אדם closes it twice, tongue at d and lips at m, and the square script draws final mem as a sealed box.
Counting. El (31) plus Yah (15) is 46. Adam is 45, one short, and one is the value of alef. The Lurianic masters got 45 another way, by spelling out the Name with alefs, and read it as Adam’s number.
You don’t have to assume clay. Ugarit pressed an alphabet into clay with a stylus in the 13th century BCE, read left to right, and there El is called ab adm, “father of man.” In clay the two words don’t even share a first letter. Ugarit wrote alef three ways depending on the vowel after it, so il takes the i-alef and adm the a-alef; ink later folded the three into one.

Then the script changed hands. The Talmud says the script was changed through Ezra, the scribe of that roll (Sanhedrin 21b). By that account, the person you imagine experimenting with a new hand is him. At Qumran the copyists wrote in his script and kept the Name in the old letters, like a seed carried in a new fruit.
