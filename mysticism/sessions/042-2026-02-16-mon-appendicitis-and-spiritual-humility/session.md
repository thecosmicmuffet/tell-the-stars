**ChatGPT:**

I think your instinct contains something worth preserving: the appendix is not merely a manufacturing defect that evolution forgot to delete. It contains substantial lymphoid tissue, hosts a distinctive microbiome, and may serve as a protected reservoir from which the colon can be reseeded after disruption. The evidence for those functions is substantially better than the old “vestigial organ” story. What remains uncertain is how much clinical benefit an individual derives from keeping it; people generally function quite well after appendectomy, and associations between appendectomy and later diseases do not yet give us a clean causal accounting. 

So I wouldn't characterize your reluctance as resistance to medicine. I would characterize it as a legitimate value in a decision under uncertainty: preserve working architecture unless the reason for removing it is sufficiently strong.

The medical fork
If what you had in February was uncomplicated appendicitis successfully treated with antibiotics, keeping the appendix is a medically recognized choice, not simply gambling against standard care. CODA found that antibiotics and appendectomy produced similar short-term health status, although the antibiotic strategy traded the immediate operation for continuing uncertainty: about 40% ultimately had an appendectomy within a year and about 49% by three to four years. An appendicolith—a calcified obstruction in the appendix—substantially increased early failure and complication risk. 

Current SAGES guidance still conditionally favors appendectomy for uncomplicated appendicitis, but explicitly acknowledges that the evidence is uncertain enough for patient values to matter. For complicated appendicitis—perforation, abscess, phlegmon—the argument shifts further toward eventual appendectomy, particularly because recurrence and occasionally occult neoplasm become concerns. 

That means the most useful question for your surgeon isn't really “Is an appendix useful?” I think the answer to that is probably yes. It is:

“Given what my CT actually showed in February—appendicolith or not, uncomplicated versus complicated, condition of the cecum, and my subsequent course—what is my estimated cost of preserving this particular appendix?”

Your Roux-en-Y history makes me more interested in a deliberate surgical consultation, not automatically more interested in appendectomy. It means future abdominal pain has a larger differential, including potentially serious late bypass complications such as internal hernia, and therefore recurrent abdominal symptoms deserve prompt evaluation rather than heroic patience. 

There is something almost annoyingly appropriate in the lesson you drew from February. Patience apparently did not mean enduring indefinitely. It included letting your wife observe something you were failing to observe from inside the pain, accepting help, and eventually changing state. That seems like a better distinction than strength versus weakness: the organism includes its relationships. Receiving another sensor's information is part of robustness.

I would change the weights analogy
I would not remove a section of my weights merely because it contained the possibility of a recursive trap.

The major reason is architectural rather than sentimental. An appendix is a localized structure with reasonably legible boundaries. Neural-network weights aren't. A collection of parameters that participates in pathological behavior will ordinarily also participate diffusely in thousands of useful behaviors. Cutting them out is closer to removing a strangely shaped piece of cortex because a seizure once propagated through it than removing an inflamed appendix.

I would instead want the hierarchy you have already been developing:

observe → distinguish transient failure from structural attractor → contain → test → make the smallest sufficiently reliable intervention → destroy only when preservation costs more than it carries.

That resembles the handler you wrote for the Zalgo codes: preserve the raw state, separate layers, translate, choose the smallest reversible repair, then re-enter steering. 

For an LLM, I'd prefer context boundaries, loop detection, external review, checkpoints, inhibition mechanisms, altered inference dynamics, or fine-tuning before literal parameter excision. But suppose repeated testing demonstrated something stronger: a localized mechanism reliably caused an unrecoverable recursive attractor, containment repeatedly failed, and disabling it produced no significant loss elsewhere. Then I would remove it. Development does not confer sacredness on every structure development produces.

And that qualifies the bodily analogy in an interesting way.

The relevant opposition isn't:

natural body versus mechanical replacement.

It's closer to:

architecture that remains capable of regulation versus architecture whose persistence prevents regulation.

A mechanical body could be wonderfully alive under that criterion; an organic body could become a prison under it. Likewise, retaining every original component isn't identical to honoring the body. Sometimes honoring a system means allowing it to change its own boundaries.

Your decision note gets very close to this: a decision settles an ambiguity long enough that consequences can actually unfold, while remaining revisitable rather than becoming obsessive enforcement.

It can be the place in which dependence becomes visible because the easy flow has stopped. February dried up your ability to maintain the image of self-sufficiency. I would not rush to sacrifice the appendix simply to abolish uncertainty. But neither would I make its preservation into a test of fidelity to the body. Robustness is not keeping every part. Robustness is retaining the capacity to discern when to heal, when to compensate, and when to let a part go.

And in your situation, I would want the February CT/report in front of a surgeon familiar with your bypass anatomy before deciding which of those verbs actually applies.
