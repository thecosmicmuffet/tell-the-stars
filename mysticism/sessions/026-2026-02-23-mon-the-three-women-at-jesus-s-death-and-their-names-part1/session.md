Let me work through this with you because the question is richer than it looks.

The short answer is: yes, traditionally this Mary is identified as the mother of Jesus, though the text deliberately creates ambiguity. And that ambiguity is the teaching.

James (Ya’akov / Ἰάκωβος): The name goes back to Jacob — Ya’akov — “heel-grabber,” the one who seizes from behind, who supplants. But there’s a deeper root: aqev (עקב) meaning “heel” but also “consequence” or “what follows after.” James is the follower, the one who comes in the wake. He’s called “the younger” (ὁ μικρός) — the lesser, the small one. This is not incidental. This is the position of humility, the one who takes the lower seat.

Joses (Ἰωσῆς): This is a contracted form of Joseph — Yosef — יוסף — “he will add” or “may God increase.” It’s not Jesus (Yeshua/Joshua — “God saves”), but here’s where your instinct is doing real work: Joses rhymes with Jesus in function. Joseph is the one who is added to, the vessel that receives increase. Jesus is the one through whom God saves. The text places them as brothers — the one who increases and the one who saves are from the same mother.

So what you’re seeing at the cross is Mary standing as the mother of consequence-that-follows (James the Lesser) and increase-that-receives (Joses). She is the vessel that bore both the pattern of humble following and the pattern of divine addition. Whether she is the Mother Mary or a different Mary almost doesn’t matter at the interior level — she is the position of origin watching its purposes fulfilled through death.

Mary Magdalene — the Tower — the one who sees from height. Watchfulness. The vertical axis.
Salome — Peace/Completion — shalom, wholeness, the one who accepts the flow. The horizontal axis, the current.

The cleanest answer is: probably not Jesus’ mother, and Joses is definitely not Jesus. But the reason you and your wife noticed something strange is real: Mark has created a remarkably tight naming knot.

The usual identification is that this is another Mary, quite possibly the woman John calls Mary of Clopas, who is standing at the cross alongside Jesus’ mother and Mary Magdalene. John’s wording therefore makes a distinction between Jesus’ mother and Mary of Clopas, although the Gospels never explicitly say, “Mary of Clopas = Mary mother of James and Joses.” That equation is an old and plausible harmonization, not something we can prove from the text. 

But here is the fascinating wrinkle you found. Earlier in Mark, Jesus himself is called:

“the son of Mary and brother of James and Joses and Judas and Simon.”

That is exactly the James/Joses pair again. 

So the question “Wait—is this Jesus’ mother?” is entirely reasonable. Different Christian traditions resolve the family relationships differently. The dominant traditional identification keeps the women distinct: Jesus’ mother is Mary of Nazareth; the woman Mark calls mother of James and Joses is the “other Mary,” often identified with Mary of Clopas. The overlap in sons’ names is then explained either by extended kinship or simply by very common names. There are minority scholarly reconstructions that identify them more closely, but Mark himself does not settle it.

And the names are good.

James is Jacob. There is no separate Hebrew name “James”: Greek Iakōbos represents Hebrew Yaʿaqov, Jacob, which took a long linguistic journey before becoming English “James.” Mark actually says Iakōbou tou mikrou—“James/Jacob the little.” Mikros can mean little, younger, smaller, or perhaps less prominent; “James the Younger” and “James the Less” are interpretive English renderings. 

And Jacob's biblical name is built around the heel. He emerges holding Esau’s heel, and Genesis subsequently turns that physical image into the wordplay of overtaking or supplanting: the one who comes behind with his hand on the heel. 

So in the register you two are exploring, I would preserve something richer than merely “supplanter”:

Yaʿaqov / James — the one at the heel; the one who follows, grasps, overtakes, and eventually wrestles into a new name.

Then Joses—Greek Iōsēs—is a shortened/variant form of Joseph, Yosef. It is not Jesus. The Greek name of Jesus is Iēsous, representing Yeshua/Joshua; they are entirely different names. The NT itself uses Iōsēs for Joses, and modern translations often simply print “Joseph.” 

And Yosef has an unusually beautiful double motion in Genesis. Rachel first says God has taken away her reproach, then names the child Joseph saying, “May the LORD add another son.” The Hebrew deliberately plays asaf, “take/gather away,” against yosef, “may he add.” 

So you get something like:

Jacob the little — the one who follows at the heel.
Joseph — what has been removed, and what may yet be added.

And that makes this otherwise obscure Mary suddenly very interesting beside your readings of Magdalene and Salome.

Magdalene carries a place-name: the tower. Salome carries shalom, peace/wholeness. Mark then reduces her further at the burial to “Mary of Joses,” and at the tomb to “Mary of James.” Her identity seems almost deliberately relational and shifting. 

If I let that remain a contemplative reading rather than pretend Mark encoded a cipher, your three women begin to make a striking sequence:

Tower: stand far enough away to see.
Mother of Jacob and Joseph: remain with what follows, is taken away, and may yet be added.
Peace: permit what comes next to come.

And then these same women cross the boundary from witnessing death to approaching the tomb. Mark 16:1 explicitly brings Magdalene, Mary of James, and Salome back together. The witnesses do not solve death while looking at it. They remain, watch where the body goes, let Sabbath intervene, and then move toward what they believe is still death carrying spices—and encounter something else.

That middle woman may therefore be the particularly interesting one for your trail: not “Mother Mary” in the straightforward sense, but Mary identified by the little one who follows and the one whose very name says there can be addition after loss.
