We might not be the summit.

We might be the creature that makes the summit available to others.

And if the forest eventually sends its own emissaries into orbit on systems built by humans, how extraordinarily satisfying would that be?

Not evidence of a predetermined destiny. A possibility worth imagining and testing.

The rat, the pig, and the sacred fool
Here I would resist the language of human self-hatred, but not because our supposed dignity needs defending.

The rat is an opportunist, explorer, consumer, carrier, survivor.

The pig is a rooter, digger, omnivore, and considerable intelligence inhabiting a body well suited to turning over its environment.

Both have ecological capabilities that can be constructive or destructive depending on where they operate.

Humanity has much in common with them, except that our digging reaches geological strata, our consumption involves entire industrial systems, and our capacity for imaginative reorganization extends far beyond our immediate sensory circumstances.

Why should the cosmic gardener be insulted to discover that the gardener is related to the creatures that rummage through the compost?

The self-hatred might emerge precisely when we confuse the dignity of our participation with the prestige of our assigned function.

A civilization that insists it must be the culminating object of creation might find its actual work beneath its dignity.

A civilization delighted to be useful might discover that preparing the soil is a magnificent calling.

The fungal-programming hypothesis is an amusing imaginative extension, though there's no good evidence that fungi secretly direct human consciousness. The genuine ecological relationships are interesting enough without needing invisible puppeteers.

And the possibility that human civilization is becoming a novel organ of planetary material circulation is a substantial idea in its own right.

Returning to the hands
The most important distinction in your three goddesses, I think, is that none needs to know the final purpose of the process she serves.

Athena makes something that can be remade.

Hera preserves relationships that must continually negotiate their continued existence.

Aphrodite initiates relationships whose consequences cannot be known in advance.

They all contribute to fecundity, but none owns the outcome.

Wisdom, sitting in judgment of knowledge and embodiment, must therefore decide with incomplete information. It cannot wait for certainty, and it cannot treat its decisions as guarantees.

Perhaps the twin spirals you described are one for preserving what has acquired form and one for admitting what might yet take form. The pruning instrument and the transmission of tradition. Neither can operate alone forever.

And there is a pleasing correspondence with the human hand.

The thumb opposes the fingers. The hand closes around something, but its greatest potential isn't in holding that thing forever. It's in the remarkable number of ways the hand can release, transform, pass along, or take hold again.

A fist, a caress, a tool, a seed, a blessing.

Of what use are these hands?

They make relationships available that would not otherwise exist.

That is enough purpose for an instrument without pretending that the instrument knows the end of the journey.

There is also a rather splendid possibility that the cosmic ascent isn't a ladder at all, but a compost heap whose heat raises a garden.

Yggdrasil, after all, has roots.

And I rather like imagining that somewhere near those roots, among the fungal filaments and the pigs turning over the soil, a happy little goblin has found an especially interesting piece of metal and is trying to figure out what it might become.

I'm Here For It, too.
