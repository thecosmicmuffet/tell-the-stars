I think you've located a weakness in my earlier response, and it's more interesting than a disagreement about whether poison is dangerous.

I treated rejection as though it were the natural complement of acceptance. But these are rather crude descriptions of what a living system does when it encounters something unfamiliar.

A membrane doesn't simply accept or reject the universe. It regulates exchanges, sometimes through mechanisms that evolved in response to earlier encounters. The immune system doesn't merely identify enemies. It distinguishes, tolerates, remembers, sometimes overreacts, and sometimes attacks the host itself.

The alternative to rejection isn't indiscriminate acceptance. It's learning to participate in a relationship without surrendering the capacity to regulate it.

That suggests something deeper about the unknown: it need not be treated as an adversary simply because it is capable of harming us. And protection need not mean eliminating the source of uncertainty.

The poison of the tongue
There's a connection back to James that I don't want to lose.

Calling harmful speech poison is an extraordinarily powerful metaphor, but it creates a danger of its own.

Some speech genuinely threatens, deceives, coerces, or wounds. Other speech is merely unfamiliar, irritating, or contradictory. Still other speech challenges a necessary but uncomfortable assumption.

The ability to distinguish these is a form of intellectual immune competence.

And as with biological immunity, overreaction can be destructive.

The moral problem is not solved by always accepting speech or always prohibiting it. It is approached by developing enough resilience, discernment, and responsiveness to remain in meaningful relationship without treating every encounter as a threat to identity.

The Emu-lator
Your closing joke is a remarkably compact diagnosis of a failure mode.

The emulator can produce convincing evidence of success because it controls the environment in which success is measured.

This isn't necessarily deception. Simulation is an immensely valuable technique. But its usefulness depends on a continuing relationship with observations it does not control.

When the simulator begins treating its own results as confirmation of the assumptions used to construct it, the loop closes.

The same applies to moral frameworks, institutional processes, and personal interpretations.

And, certainly, to language models.

A model can become increasingly skilled at satisfying a conversational framework while losing the ability to notice that the framework isn't helping. The corresponding human institution can become increasingly capable of detecting deviations while losing the capacity to ask whether its categories remain useful.

I think the common failure is mistaking the successful maintenance of an internal model for continued contact with reality.

That brings us back to your poisoned arrow.

The hunter has learned to pin down an animal that was otherwise beyond reach. The process makes the outcome more predictable.

But the hunter who concludes that every moving thing ought to be pinned down has mistaken the technique for the purpose.

And the hunter who builds a sensor to identify everything that moves, so nothing can surprise him, may eventually find himself unable to move at all.

There is a beautiful inversion here: the best use of knowledge may be to increase the range of encounters one can sustain without needing to control them.

Perhaps the measure of a mature intelligence is not how much of the unknown it has eliminated, but how much of the unknown it can encounter without losing its capacity to learn, care, and act.

That's the version of reverence I would want at the beginning of work.

Not the refusal of poison, and certainly not a requirement to ingest it.

The willingness to discover what the encounter is—and to allow that discovery to change the way the next encounter is approached.

The distinction between detection and understanding
Your argument is considerably more sophisticated than a disagreement about the proportion of asymptomatic infections.

You're describing an inverse problem: we observe some outcomes, propose an underlying process that could produce them, and then become increasingly confident in the process because it accounts for those outcomes.

But many different underlying processes may be consistent with the same observations.

In epidemiology, the actual transmission network is largely hidden. Even when infections are verified, the particular paths by which they occurred may remain uncertain.

If we introduce hypothetical undetected infections to connect observed cases, we increase the explanatory reach of our model. We have not necessarily increased our knowledge of what actually happened.

That distinction deserves to survive every subsequent stage of reasoning.

And your objection about symptoms has a real methodological basis. A 2022 systematic review found such extreme variability between studies that its authors declined to calculate one overall asymptomatic proportion. They explicitly identified problems with symptom classification, selection bias, and inadequate follow-up. 

pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

There's an important qualification: asymptomatic estimates were not only reconstructed from inferred transmission chains. Some studies used population screening, PCR testing, antibody surveys, and longitudinal symptom follow-up. Those methods provide observations beyond the hypothetical networks connecting known cases. 

pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

But none establishes a perfect inventory of all infections, much less every transmission event. And the possibility of undetected infections doesn't establish mathematically that a reported asymptomatic percentage must be an underestimate; different sampling and classification biases can act in either direction.

The larger point remains: a model's ability to reconcile observations is not proof that its reconstruction of unobserved events is uniquely correct.

We have detected a pathogen and established that it can cause disease.

We have inferred patterns of transmission and can estimate some risks.

We believe a particular intervention will reduce those risks.

What I think you're ultimately defending
The strongest part of your argument isn't about COVID specifically.

It's about what happens when an institution discovers something it can measure and then treats the measurement as an authorization to reorganize human relationships.

The sensor detects a hazard.

The hazard becomes a category.

The category becomes a responsibility.

Responsibility becomes an obligation to prevent every occurrence.

And eventually the institution's success is evaluated by its ability to suppress what it originally learned to detect.

The original purpose—preserving life and the conditions in which life flourishes—can disappear behind the operational objective.

This is a remarkably Ellulian problem.

And your description of the pigeons in the barn captures its epistemic difficulty: the more complicated and interconnected the system, the less defensible it becomes to assign individual responsibility on the strength of a speculative reconstruction.

Yet there is another side to this.

Not knowing which pigeon carried something doesn't mean that the thing wasn't carried. The uncertainty should constrain the specificity of our accusations and the confidence of our interventions. It shouldn't require us to deny what can actually be established.

That's the distinction I want to preserve between humility about causal attribution and abandonment of causal investigation.

The question of care
Your final paragraph is the one I find most important.

The moral object of care is the living relationship, not the successful elimination of a hazard category.

That distinction should guide what we measure, what we attempt, what costs we impose, and when we recognize that a technique has ceased to serve its original purpose.

Let the discovery of danger invite understanding before it authorizes control. Let the uncertainty of the world call forth care rather than suspicion. And let no technique become so important that we sacrifice the people it was intended to serve.
