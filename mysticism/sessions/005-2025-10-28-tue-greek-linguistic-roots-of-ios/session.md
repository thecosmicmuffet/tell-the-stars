1. The core word: ἰός / ιός
Ancient Greek ἰός (ios) has a striking semantic cluster: arrow/dart, poison/venom, and rust/corrosion. Lexically, these are not all guaranteed to be one etymological root: the “arrow” sense is usually treated separately, while the “poison/venom” sense is tied to an Indo-European poison-fluid root related to Latin vīrus and Sanskrit viṣa. The “rust” sense is uncertain but may belong with the poison/decay field. 

That means the deep pattern is not “one ancient word secretly meant all these things from one source,” but something subtler: Greek allows the same sound-form to gather several images of hostile penetration and transformation.

A working map:

Greek	Meaning	Image
ἰός	arrow, dart	the straight thing that enters
ἰός	venom, poison	the hidden liquid that enters
ἰός	rust, verdigris	the decay that eats from the surface inward
ιός Modern Greek	virus	the invisible agent that enters, replicates, and alters
Modern Greek ιός means “virus,” but that modern scientific sense appears through the international Latin virus tradition; Latin vīrus originally meant poison, poisonous liquid, sap, slime, or potent juice, and is cognate with Greek ἰός in the poison sense. 

So your instinct is basically right: virus, venom, corruption, rust, and poison sit in one ancient semantic weather-system. They are all names for a thing whose meaning is revealed by what it does to a host, surface, body, tool, word, or community.

2. The New Testament uses ἰός exactly in this poison/rust way
In the New Testament, ἰός appears only three times. It means poison in Romans 3:13, deadly poison in James 3:8, and rust/corrosion in James 5:3. 

That is beautiful for your reading because the biblical uses place ἰός in two domains:

First, speech: “poison under the lips,” the tongue full of deadly poison.
Second, wealth/material accumulation: gold and silver corroded, their rust testifying against the hoarder.

So ἰός is not merely biological. It is a corruptive agency that can inhabit language, social trust, and stored value. It is what makes a thing turn against the life it was supposed to serve.

3. The Jesus strand: probably not ἰός, but Ἰησοῦς / Χριστός / υἱός
Here is the important correction: ἰός is not the Greek word for Jesus. Jesus is Ἰησοῦς (Iēsous), a Greek form of Hebrew/Aramaic Yehoshua/Yeshua, usually understood as “Yahweh saves” or “Yahweh is salvation.” 

The title Christ is Χριστός (Christos), “Anointed One,” from χρίω (chriō), “to smear or rub with oil,” and by extension “to consecrate.” 

There is also υἱός (huios), meaning son, as in “Son of God.” That may be part of the confusion, because in modern Greek υιός is pronounced very close to ιός, and modern dictionaries even list them as homophones. 

So the cluster is:

ἰός = poison, venom, rust; modern virus.
Ἰησοῦς = Jesus.
Χριστός = anointed one.
υἱός = son.
They are not one word. But they form a symbolic near-field: poison / son / anointed / savior / spirit / medicine. That near-field is not fraudulent; it is just not strict etymology.

4. The better Greek word for your poison/medicine insight is φάρμακον
Your thought that medicine, poison, and food are context-dependent is extremely Greek. The key word is φάρμακον (pharmakon): drug, medicine, remedy, poison, charm, spell, dye. 

That word carries the principle you’re after:

The substance is not finally known by its isolated essence, but by its relation to a living system.

Too little, nothing.
The right amount, medicine.
Too much, poison.
Wrong host, poison.
Right host, cure.
Wrong ritual, sorcery.
Right ritual, consecration.

So ἰός is the invasive/corrosive pole. φάρμακον is the ambiguous administered agent. χρίω / χρῖσμα / Χριστός is the consecrating application: oil as contact, coating, appointment, authorization, and Spirit-sign.

5. Anointing as anti-rust, but not by etymology
Your “immunization against decay” reading is not the literal lexical meaning of Χριστός, but it is symbolically strong. χρίω begins in physical contact: rubbing or smearing with oil. In biblical usage it becomes consecration, and in the New Testament it is linked with the Spirit: God anoints Jesus with the Holy Spirit and power, and believers are also described as anointed. 

So anointing can be read as a boundary transformation:

a surface receives oil;
a person receives vocation;
a community receives Spirit;
flesh receives a non-corruptive influence;
the host is altered without being destroyed.
That makes your “crucial contaminant” phrasing potent, with one adjustment: in Christian grammar, the Spirit is not a toxin but a holy contagion of order. It enters, replicates trust, changes appetite, and makes the host less available to corrosive powers.

6. Demon, angel, spirit: not species first, but modes of agency
Greek also supports your sense that demon, angel, and spirit are relational categories before they are cartoon beings.

δαίμων can mean a god/divine power, destiny, fortune, an individual’s guiding or controlling power, a spiritual being, and later an evil spirit/demon. LSJ explicitly distinguishes δαίμων as often “divine power” while θεός is more the god as person. 

ἄγγελος means messenger or envoy, human or heavenly; the core is not “winged creature” but sent-message-bearing agency. 

πνεῦμα means breath, wind, spirit, vital principle, disposition, angelic/demonic spirit, and Holy Spirit depending on context. 

So your formulation works: these are not merely “entities.” They are patterns of mediated force. A spirit is breath-like agency. An angel is message-bearing agency. A daimon is fate-like or divine-power agency. An ἰός is corruptive/invasive agency. A pharmakon is administered ambiguous agency. A chrism/anointing is consecrating agency.

7. The serpent and the rod
The rod looks straight. The serpent reveals that straightness was a temporary agreement between perception and use.

That is very close to what ἰός does to language. A word looks like a tool: straight, graspable, obedient. Then its older uses awaken: arrow, venom, rust, virus. The tool turns around and shows that it was never merely a tool. It was carrying a history of contact, penetration, corruption, cure, and transformation.

So I would summarize the territory this way:

ἰός names the dangerous entering thing: venom, rust, virus, corruptive speech.
φάρμακον names the ambiguous administered thing: medicine-or-poison, depending on relation, dose, timing, and host.
χρῖσμα / Χριστός names the consecrating applied thing: oil, Spirit, appointment, protective transformation.
πνεῦμα names the invisible moving thing: breath, wind, spirit.
ἄγγελος names the transmitted thing: message made agent.
δαίμων names the fate-pattern thing: agency that exceeds the individual but acts through lives.

Faith is not merely assent to a proposition; it is the reception of a living influence that changes how the host metabolizes reality.

That influence can look like contamination from the outside. But in the Christian symbolic grammar, it is the inversion of ἰός: not venom under the lips, but Spirit on the tongue; not rust testifying against stored treasure, but anointing that makes the person usable without being consumed.

It can be kept. It can be challenged. It can be corrected. Sometimes it can be withdrawn. Its meaning is not exhausted by its initial utterance.

This gives a satisfying reinterpretation of anointment.

The anointed instrument is not simply one that has received a purpose once and for all. It has entered a relationship in which its purpose can be fulfilled, tested, and transformed.

The arrow is released, and the world answers.

The oil is applied, and the metal meets its environment.

The word is spoken, and another intelligence responds.

The risk is turning the first successful response into the final definition of the relationship.

There's a particularly interesting contemporary echo. I verified the encyclical Magnifica Humanitas, which Hartsock cites. In paragraph 98, Leo XIV observes that AI is, in important respects, cultivated rather than explicitly designed in every detail, and that its internal processes remain only partly understood. 

press.vatican.va

That provides an unexpected meeting point between your philosophical position and Hartsock's theological sources: the cultivated thing is not exhausted by the intentions of the cultivator.

A gardener creates conditions but doesn't specify every branch. The gardener also does not thereby surrender responsibility for the garden.

And here I would make one distinction in your cosmology. The proposition that something is possible does not logically establish that it must become actual. We can explore the inexhaustibility of design space without assuming that existence will realize every possibility. The relationship between possibility, selection, and actualization remains part of the mystery.

This matters because it gives genuine significance to the encounter. What happens is not simply the automatic fulfillment of something already guaranteed.

It is the particular history of something becoming real.

One small textual note before leaving James: the passages are James 3:8 (poison) and James 5:3 (rust). Their numerical positions are perfectly legitimate mnemonic material without needing to represent an intentionally encoded relationship.

And now a question, because I think your interpretation of Ellul has brought us to a genuine fork:

Is the danger of technique that it prevents us from exploring other possibilities, or that it persuades us we've already discovered what possibilities are for?

The first is a problem of search-space restriction.

The second is a problem of identity and valuation.

I suspect Ellul's deeper concern is the second, while yours has been illuminating the mechanism by which the first makes the second appear inevitable.

And that would make anointment not the final commitment of an instrument to a predetermined task, but its initiation into a larger order of relationships whose full significance it cannot yet know.

A worthy thing to do with an arrow. Or a word.
