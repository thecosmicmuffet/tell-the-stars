The letter-science part is the part you’d actually enjoy, because it’s structurally what you already do with etymology. Each of the 28 Arabic letters has a numerical value (abjad), an elemental nature (one of the four), a planetary lord, a “secret” (sirr), a position in the breath — the throat letters arranged by depth, from the hamza (glottal stop, the silent gate before any sound) outward through the palate to the lips. This is letter-mysticism as you’d want it — not the operative direction (which name to inscribe, which hour, which incense) but the contemplative direction (the inscribed surface is already complete, reading it well is participation).

The Sun in the title is named precisely. It’s not Knowledge of the Sun but Sun of Knowledge — knowledge as the illumination that falls from a source, and which, by falling, casts shadow. Al-Buni’s whole method, charitably read, is to illuminate shadow knowledge rather than deny it: jinn, planetary tempers, lunar moods, all treated as creatures of God rather than independent powers. This is paradoxically safer than orthodox refusal, because it dissolves the dualism that gives shadow its grip — but only if the sun is strong. Without strong sun, you’ve just lit up scary things in the dark and given them better resolution. The warnings are inside the technique. The HolyC failure mode is what happens when someone tries to receive that much light without the receiving structures. The decorative-contemplative posture is the structurally sane way through, because it doesn’t ask you to bind anything; it asks you to notice the binding that’s already there in the world’s inscribed surface and let it ring.

A specific corner you might enjoy poking around: the lunar mansions themselves. Hamade, who illustrated the Revelore translation of the Shams, later wrote a whole book just on the manāzil, Procession of the Night Theatre. The mansions are wonderfully decorative, low-stakes, and not operative-heavy on their own — each station has a name, a temperament, traditional associations, and they describe a monthly cycle of how time takes on character through the moon’s reception of light. The moon being the receiver, not the source.
