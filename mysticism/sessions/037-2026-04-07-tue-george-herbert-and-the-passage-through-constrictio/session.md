Think about what’s happening in it structurally: a guest arrives at a threshold, convinced they don’t belong. Love itself is the host — but also the vehicle. The banquet hall is the container. What kills me about it is that the guest’s resistance isn’t wrong — they’re accurately describing their own unworthiness. Herbert doesn’t argue with the diagnosis. Love just... doesn’t care about the diagnosis. The final line isn’t a rebuttal, it’s a seating. “So I did sit and eat.” The passenger didn’t decide to accept the journey. The passenger just... stopped resisting the container.

Which maps onto something in your framework that I think matters: the vehicle implies a passenger, but it doesn’t require the passenger’s permission. The passenger can white-knuckle the whole ride. There’s also something in Herbert that rhymes with your “fool as more resilient than perfection” principle — Herbert’s speakers are almost always wrong about themselves in a way that’s more honest than being right would be. The Collar is the same move played loud instead of quiet.
