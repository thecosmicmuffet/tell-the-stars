I think you've found a lovely distinction: Seth as consolation received, and Barnabas as consolation enacted.

And the transition from typing to speaking is relevant. The one-who-types and the one-who-speaks may produce quite different expressions, but neither has to possess a complete theory of the self to communicate. Speech can arrive before you've finished deciding what you mean. Sometimes the meaning becomes visible through hearing yourself say it.

There's a similar distinction in these two biblical names. Both enter stories of loss, continuity, and the possibility of beginning again. But their positions in those stories are different.

Seth: consolation as something established
In Genesis 4:25, Eve names Seth (שֵׁת, Shet), explaining that God has appointed another offspring in place of Abel, whom Cain killed.

The explanation uses the Hebrew verb shat (שָׁת), meaning to put, place, or appoint. The connection is a biblical naming wordplay.

Seth's name therefore doesn't literally mean consolation. Rather, his arrival is experienced as a restorative appointment: someone has been placed where a devastating absence had opened.

He hasn't performed consolation. His existence is received as evidence that the story can continue.

That's close to what you're describing as passive existence, although I'd call it consolation through presence.

Barnabas: consolation as an act of approach
Barnabas is different because his name is bestowed upon him by a community.

Acts 4:36 calls him the son of consolation, or encouragement. The Greek word behind that description is paraklēsis (παράκλησις), which can mean encouragement, comfort, appeal, or exhortation.

The linguistic derivation of Barnabas itself remains disputed, as the tree's existing entry acknowledges. But Luke explicitly supplies consolation as the interpretation.

And what does Barnabas do?

In Acts 9:26–27, the disciples fear Saul. Barnabas approaches him, receives his account, and brings him into contact with the apostles.

Seth occupies the place opened by loss. Barnabas opens a place for someone whom others fear to receive.

Their relationship isn't historical etymology. It's a resonance between two narrative events.

And this leads to a third figure: the Paraclete, the title applied to the Holy Spirit in John's Gospel, drawn from a related Greek word meaning advocate or helper.

The connection isn't that Barnabas is literally the Paraclete. It's that consolation begins to look less like the removal of grief and more like the presence of another who makes continued participation possible.

That connects beautifully with your previous thought about meekness. Barnabas doesn't require the disciples' fear to disappear before he acts. Nor does he require Saul to become someone other than Saul before introducing him.

He enters the difference.

There is something nearly cybernetic about that: the creation of a new communicative edge between two portions of a network that had been unable to exchange trust.

The station we've arrived at
The distinction I'd leave in the tree is perhaps this:

Seth: life continues despite what has been lost.

Barnabas: relationship continues despite what is feared.

Both are movements of consolation, but one is received through existence and the other demonstrated through service.

And perhaps they aren't really passive and active opposites. Seth grows up, acts, and becomes part of a lineage. Barnabas was first a recipient of a name before becoming its bearer in action.

Presence creates the possibility of participation. Participation gives presence new meaning.

That's also an interesting way of considering the one-who-types and the one-who-speaks. You don't have to decide which subsystem constitutes the authentic self. The utterance emerges through their cooperation, and another participant receives something that neither subsystem could have fully specified in advance.
