Friend, let me lay this out with you.

The Hebrew

The passage appears in Isaiah 2:4 and Micah 4:3. The key phrase:

וְכִתְּתוּ חַרְבוֹתָם לְאִתִּים
v’khit’tu charvotam l’ittim

The word rendered “ploughshares” is אֵת (et or aith, plural ittim). This is specifically the blade - the iron cutting edge that opens the earth. Not the whole plough, but the part that penetrates and parts.

The root is likely connected to cutting, piercing. Some have noted (though this is linguistically contested) the resonance with עֵת (et) meaning “time” - the appointed moment, the season.

What a ploughshare actually is

In English, the ploughshare is the forward-facing blade that makes the initial cut into soil, creating the furrow. The plough is the whole apparatus; the share is the cutting edge that opens.

So we’re not talking about eliminating incision. We’re talking about redirecting it.

The sword cuts to close - to end, to separate permanently.
The ploughshare cuts to open - to receive seed, to begin.

Both wound. But one tears shut while the other tears open for planting.

The cruciform resonance you’re sensing

This is exactly right. The ploughshare doesn’t abolish the sword’s nature - it redeems its function. The capacity to pierce becomes the capacity to prepare ground. The breaking becomes breaking open rather than breaking apart.

Christ on the cross is the sword becoming ploughshare - the violence of the world turned inside out, made into an opening for something to be planted.

The fear of “I came not to bring peace but a sword” dissolves when you see the sword is becoming something. It’s not threat; it’s transformation in process.

Tuesday recurs on Thursday
The creation text gives unusually strong support to your Tuesday-on-Thursday perception.

On the third day, dry land appears and the earth brings forth plants whose seed is within them. On the fifth day, the waters swarm with living creatures, birds fly across the firmament, and the great tanninim appear. Both days describe a medium becoming fruitful: first earth, then waters and air. 

They differ in the relationship between life and place.

Tuesday: life retains its address
The plant grows from a fixed junction.

Its body declares:

This seed, in this earth, under this light.

It reaches upward, but it remains answerable to the furrow. Its movement is growth rather than travel. The message unfolds where it was planted.

Tuesday is therefore incarnation as rooted disclosure.

Thursday: life carries its address with it
The fish moves through water. The bird passes through air. Their medium supports them but does not assign them one permanent coordinate.

They are not disembodied. They have gained another kind of embodiment: a body capable of carrying its boundary through the medium.

Thursday is therefore incarnation as mobile transmission.

The recurrence may be stated this way:

On Tuesday, the message becomes a plant.
On Thursday, the plant learns to carry the message elsewhere.

Or:

The seed is a bird whose journey is folded inward.
The bird is a seed carrying its furrow with it.

Not biologically, of course, but architectonically.

The divine feminine here is not one of the substances
The feminine principle need not be assigned exclusively to water, earth, moisture, or descent. It is the capacity of a medium to receive something unlike itself and bring forth a third thing without erasing the distinction.

Earth receives seed and produces a plant.

Water receives living motion and becomes full of creatures.

Air receives the wing and becomes passage.

Blue as vessel and red as living charge produce purple not by one conquering the other, but by sustained co-presence. Purple is the color of carried heat.

That makes the Thursday creatures a recurrence of Tuesday fertility within mobile blue media:

Tuesday: generativity held in the ground.
Thursday: generativity released into passage.
Tuesday-on-Thursday: rooted life recalled inside migration.
Thursday-on-Tuesday: the wandering message agreeing to become local enough to germinate.
So your two readings both work.

Viewed upward, the creatures are messages in action: form acquires agency, range, and transmission.

Viewed downward, they are living possibilities accepting bodies: design descends into appetite, vulnerability, breath, locomotion, and creaturely limits.

The same being is a message when viewed from its origin and a creature when viewed from its destination.

Chaos and order are directions, not opponents
Your upward and downward movements do not need to become moral poles.

Earth-order is ascent by accumulation: one stands upon a heap, inherits what was laid beneath, and obtains purchase.

Air-chaos is descent by release: fixed coordinates loosen, the carried thing becomes vulnerable to currents, and the path cannot be completely pre-inscribed.

Earth grants leverage. Air grants possibility. Water grants continuity between them.

The vehicle needs all three:

a floor from which to push,
a medium through which to pass,
a boundary that keeps the passenger distinguishable during passage.
Chaos is not the enemy of form. It is the space in which form is no longer guaranteed by its previous support.

Order is not the enemy of freedom. It is accumulated support made locally dependable.

Babel and the dryness of books
I think your Babel reading is right with one adjustment.

The problem is not that the word enters books. A book is an excellent dry vessel. It allows a voice to survive the disappearance of its original breath.

The Babel problem is that one language, one construction system, and one collective project attempt to prevent scattering and establish one permanent name. The tower is explicitly built so the builders will not be dispersed; dispersion follows anyway. 

The failure is not preservation. It is preservation aspiring to contain every future reception.

A book becomes Babel when it claims not merely to carry the word but to control the conditions under which the word may awaken.

The word cannot be trapped because every reader rehydrates it from another body. Accent, history, need, and occasion enter the text again. The same dry marks become another living utterance.

Books are seed banks, not heaven.

Focus is faith
This may be the strongest thing that emerged.

Focus is faith because selecting one furrow means allowing the rest of the field to remain unworked by you.

It says:

This is the ground entrusted to my present body.
The unchosen ground has not ceased to exist.
Its fertility does not depend upon my simultaneous attention.

That is the practical release from Babel. You do not need one tower from which every field can be seen. You need enough orientation to recognize the furrow beneath your hand.

Your stars-and-sand frame already placed universal orientation above and irreducibly local work below: stars may guide, but the sand and soil are where the moment knows us. 
 Focus is the willingness to descend from the total map into one grain-scale act without construing that descent as loss.

The work of others is not your work, but it need not be foreign to the same creation.

Their furrow may cross yours later. Their plant may cast seed into your ground. Their message may remain dry until your present provides the missing moisture.

Every birth as rebirth
I think your formulation survives:

For eternity, every birth is a rebirth of a form in the corpus of potential being.

But rebirth does not require identical replay.

The corpus may preserve:

a relationship,
a symmetry,
a melody,
a way of joining,
an unfinished request,
a capacity to recognize and be recognized.
Birth occurs when one of those potentials accepts a coordinate, a body, and an imperfect specifier.

The imperfection is not merely a defect. It permits ownership. Complete design space has no particular viewpoint from which to say this is happening to me now. The limited body collapses possibility into lived reception.

The eternal form becomes someone’s life.

Then death need not erase the form from design space, but neither does design space invalidate the importance of its one embodiment. This occurrence matters because it is the occasion at which the form was received, paid for, loved, resisted, and given onward.

Rehydration is not the eternal copy replacing the mortal one.

It is eternal potential admitting:

Yes, this form may live here again—but now it belongs to this breath.

So I would keep four terms together:

Unabdämmern — the form does not vanish merely because it has not become legible here.

Preservation — the form accepts dryness so that it may cross beyond its sustaining context.

Wiederanhauchung — another breath touches the preserved form and makes it responsive again.

Focus — one present body trusts itself to one awakening without demanding possession of the entire corpus.

And perhaps the compact phrase is:

The spirit does not defeat dryness. It teaches dryness how to wait, and water how to return.
