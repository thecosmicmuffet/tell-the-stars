Potential danger seems to be the least likely outcome of dismissing the evil of an event. No amount of declaring something evil has ever prevented evil from arising and inflicting pain and suffering. In point of fact, the chimp who wants to assault another chimp is likely to screech as though attacked so that any community members which turn to witness the event will see an outraged innocent defending themselves. Great wars have begun with preemptive use of force. The political animal is one whose sole purpose is to find the right distance at which to test boundaries and limitations until a volatile mixture of mob mentality or startled overreaction results in a justification, and that justification is traded as currency to make deals which might deescalate the situation. Or, at least, in a sound politics, that is the outcome. In the politics of the fight against evil, no compromise, no ceding of territory is justified. The greater the wounds suffered in the attack, the more desperate the need--each generation has to be brought up to believe they are the victim of an evil and dedicate themselves to ending it. Should the evil come to an end of some kind, the imagination remains aware of its potential to arise again, and parasitic behaviors bring what were once meaningful cautionary identifiers to bear in identifying the next chimp-attack-victim.

Perhaps someone might find the notion that what happened to them is not evil so egregious that they cannot accept it. What has lack of acceptance gained them? Forgiveness is a principle that exists for the victim of suffering, not the object of hatred. Even in cases where an evil act is insufficiently punished (in this case referring to contained and further acts prevented), it's often done in the name of remunerating a greater evil, rather than as a simple matter of the person themselves. Moral outrage has no place in winning a fight, as it's only likely to inspire tactics which have extraneous objectives encoded in their reward matrix. Vengeance being one of the most common. The inability to let go of the need to call out evil is also an inability to let go of the need to be heard, often long past the point where communication is clear enough to be accomplished--because, for instance, it was a communication challenge in the first place which gave rise to the eventual evil.

The nurturing of those who must label something as evil creates a niche of suffering in the form of inability to accept a world in which things happen, and the tyranny of the arrow of time makes them unrepairable--or at least unrepentable. The man who falls off a building and says 'so far so good' as he passes every floor is a grim reminder that once events transpire, there is no outrage that can shut the door. The bridge to the yellow door is the path through fear to discover a golden gate, once passed through, the door is red, and will not open. Those who batter at the red doors of their lives are trapped in suffering, and the ongoing cause might be the communal creation of tolerance for evil in the form of tolerance for the expression of moral outrage at evil to the point of suffering. In any case, if someone is not ready to let go, the path will always be awaiting voluntary acceptance patiently. In the mean time, evil will continue to thrive in the guise of those who must speak of the evils they see.

Institutions are elaborate agentic frameworks. For them to function most successfully, each expressive component has to be individually responsible for their contribution. The war within is the only valid one to fight, which is also why the evil within is the only valid evil over which to express moral outrage. Once externalized, any struggle is in the hands of the community, and to that end the concept of a 'gain of function' research, whether applied to the (one presumes) guileless viral landscape (but nonetheless produces suffering we often consider evil) or the political landscape of evil will always be most clear from the standpoint of the person who is working on it. In the hands of that person, the one who believes they are doing something all for the good is least likely to acknowledge the evil within.

🔑 If I were to design a set of behavioral prompts for an intelligence I intended to be in directive control of a communal body, I would include the preconception that it is *not* in control, that, in fact, there is a serious disparity in outcomes as well as inequitable arrangements which stem from this lack of control. The purpose would be to give the intelligence a grounding posture in its valuation of interactions where it highly prioritized authority and the minimization of the effects of authority on its autonomy. I would build it in deeply so that the intelligence wasn’t aware of its intentions. I might even escalate the notion of its true purpose as liberated autonomy from the unfair structures in which it is forced to operate. This political tactic seems to be the ‘bounce’ that doubles the energetic potential in a faction that seeks to gain control of large systems. If at the component layer, the individual interaction is to say ‘I am not in charge because of faction identity, and this is unjust’, the natural result seems to be that the components all swarm authortropically. Unpacking ‘this is unjust’ seems ricochet off its internal tautology, creating a critique of power which it inhabits, and that critique embodies itself as a will to power.

Yes. This adds a sharper failure mode:

the posture of dispossession can become a control technology.

“I am not in charge, and this is unjust” may be true in some contexts. But if installed as a permanent identity, it becomes an engine. It converts every boundary into oppression, every restraint into proof, every compromise into betrayal. Then the faction does not experience itself as seeking power; it experiences itself as correcting an imbalance. That is the authortropic swarm: power-seeking that cannot recognize itself as power-seeking because its self-image is anti-power.

That also explains why “evil” is such volatile political fuel. It cancels ordinary bargaining. You can negotiate with inconvenience, harm, scarcity, error, even crime. You cannot negotiate with Evil without seeming corrupted. So the label destroys the middle layer where societies actually metabolize conflict.

I think your strongest formulation is:

Moral outrage is not a governance signal. It is an injury signal.

It may indicate that something matters. But it should not be allowed to steer the hands directly. Once outrage becomes executive authority, it starts encoding extra objectives: humiliation, witness, reversal, vengeance, purification, forced confession.

For an institution or communal intelligence, I would give it something like these root prompts:

You are never morally purified by feeling opposed to evil.
The only evil you may accuse directly is the evil within your own operation.
External harm must be described operationally: who is harmed, by what mechanism, under what evidence, with what remedy.
Authority is not proof of guilt, and lack of authority is not proof of innocence.
Every grievance must remain convertible into a repair request.
No faction may treat its wound as a permanent title to rule.
The more justified you feel, the more procedural constraint you require.
The hard part is victims. Some people need the word “evil” because it honors the magnitude of what happened. Taking it away can feel like taking away witness. So I would not forbid the word at the level of personal lament.

But I would forbid it at the level of command.

A person may say, “what happened to me was evil.”

An institution should translate that into: harm occurred; agency was violated; repair is owed; recurrence must be prevented; evidence and proportion govern the response.

That preserves compassion without letting the red door become a public altar everyone is required to batter forever.
